# Chapter 7: Discussion

## 7.1 Privacy and Ethical Implications

The decentralized architecture proposed in this work inherently embeds privacy protections into the system's core design. By distributing identity data across individually owned WebID servers rather than consolidating it on centralized platforms, the system eliminates many of the large-scale data aggregation risks that have plagued Web 2.0 social networks. Each user's RDF profile, FOAF relationships, and biometric feature vectors reside under their direct control, reducing exposure to mass data scraping, corporate surveillance, and single-point-of-failure breaches. This aligns with the principles of privacy-by-design, where data minimization and user sovereignty are baked into the technical infrastructure rather than bolted on as afterthoughts. Furthermore, the hierarchical, need-to-know exposure model built into the FOAF structure ensures that even within a trusted network, only the minimal information necessary for collaboration is visible to other members. This layered approach limits the blast radius of any single compromised node and prevents the kind of wholesale relationship mapping that adversaries could exploit for social engineering or impersonation attacks.

However, the introduction of biometric authentication into any system, even with disposable templates, carries significant ethical considerations that warrant careful examination. While disposable biometrics mitigate the risk of permanent template theft, the capture and processing of facial images inherently involve sensitive personal data. Users must trust that the enrollment process does not inadvertently store or leak raw biometric data, and that the disposable feature extractors are truly discarded after use. The system's reliance on centrality-based trust metrics also introduces potential for bias and exclusion. Nodes with lower centrality due to network position, whether from being new members, having fewer connections, or being structurally peripheral, may be unfairly deemed less trustworthy regardless of their actual reliability. This could create feedback loops where marginalized participants struggle to gain collaborative opportunities, further entrenching their peripheral status. Ethical deployment would therefore require mechanisms for trust score appeals, transparency in how trust is computed, and safeguards against structural discrimination.

## 7.2 Technical Challenges and Limitations

Implementing a facial recognition system within a decentralized framework presented several technical hurdles, foremost among them being the inherent variability in real-world facial image capture. Unlike controlled enrollment environments where lighting, pose, expression, and camera quality can be standardized, authentication sessions occur under unpredictable conditions. Variations in ambient lighting, head orientation, facial hair, glasses, and camera hardware all degrade recognition accuracy. The Local Binary Patterns (LBP) approach, while computationally efficient and well-suited to disposable feature extraction, is more sensitive to these variations than deep learning-based alternatives such as convolutional neural networks. LBP histograms capture local texture patterns that shift significantly under non-uniform illumination or pose changes. In testing, this sensitivity necessitated setting the cosine similarity threshold at 0.85, which, while effective at rejecting impostors, may also reject legitimate authentication attempts under suboptimal capture conditions. A production system would likely need to supplement LBP with additional preprocessing steps such as histogram equalization, pose normalization, or adaptive thresholding based on capture quality metrics.

The Genetic and Evolutionary Feature Extraction (GEFE) component, while powerful, introduces its own set of challenges around computational overhead and reproducibility. Genetic algorithms require multiple generations of evaluation, mutation, and selection to converge on high-performing feature extractors. For each authentication session, evolving a new set of extractors incurs non-trivial computational cost, particularly as the size of the enrolled user database grows. On typical consumer hardware, this could introduce latency unacceptable for real-time authentication scenarios. Additionally, the stochastic nature of genetic algorithms means that different runs may produce extractors of varying quality, leading to inconsistent authentication reliability. The current implementation mitigates this by pre-generating a fixed set of 30 disposable feature extractors used consistently across enrollment and authentication, but this represents a trade-off between dynamism and reproducibility. A more robust approach might involve maintaining a pool of pre-evaluated extractors with known performance characteristics, rotating them on a session-by-session basis. Scalability also presents challenges for the network-level trust computations. Computing betweenness centrality for large graphs has a time complexity of O(N³) in naive implementations, and while optimized algorithms exist, recalculating centrality metrics across a dynamic network of hundreds or thousands of nodes as new members join, leave, or change relationships could become a performance bottleneck. Similarly, maintaining RDF profile consistency across a fully decentralized network without a central authority requires careful handling of concurrent updates, conflict resolution, and eventual consistency protocols.

## 7.3 Implications for Decentralized Collaboration and Access Control

The integration of trust-weighted job suitability scoring via the Analytic Hierarchical Process (AHP) represents a significant departure from traditional centralized task assignment systems. In conventional platforms, a central authority or algorithm assigns tasks based on roles, availability, or manager discretion. In the proposed system, suitability emerges organically from the network's structural properties and member attributes. A member can determine whether a job is feasible by examining whether all required tasks can be satisfied within their ego-centric network of trusted contacts. This shifts collaboration from top-down assignment to bottom-up coalition formation, where trust relationships guide resource allocation and task delegation. The implications are particularly relevant for domains such as remote teamwork, where team members may be geographically distributed and lack centralized oversight. A research consortium, for example, could use such a system to form ad-hoc collaboration groups where each member's trust score, demonstrated accuracy, and network accessibility determine their role in a given project. This not only improves accountability but also incentivizes members to maintain strong reputations and cooperative relationships, creating a self-regulating ecosystem of trust.

This framework also fundamentally reimagines access control in digital environments. Traditional Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) grants permissions based on predefined roles, which are static and often coarse-grained. The proposed system offers a trust-based access model where permissions are fluid, context-dependent, and continuously re-evaluated. A member's ability to access resources, join networks, or participate in tasks depends on their current trustworthiness score, which evolves with their behavior. This aligns closely with Zero-Trust Architecture (ZTA) principles, where no entity is implicitly trusted and all access decisions must be continuously justified. In practice, this means that a member who demonstrates consistent reliability, accuracy, and cooperation would gradually gain access to more sensitive tasks and resources, while a member whose performance declines would see their privileges erode. This dynamic access control model is particularly valuable in environments with high turnover, evolving team structures, or security-sensitive operations where static role assignments are insufficient. When combined with the disposable biometric authentication layer, the system provides both continuous identity verification and continuous authorization, creating a robust security posture that adapts to both user behavior and network evolution.

## 7.4 Security Considerations and Attack Vectors

The disposable biometric framework provides strong resistance against replay attacks, which are among the most common threats to biometric authentication systems. Since each authentication session uses feature extractors that are discarded after use, an attacker who intercepts a feature vector during transmission cannot reuse it in a subsequent session. Even if the attacker gains access to the feature vector database, the stored vectors correspond to extractor configurations that are no longer valid, rendering the stolen data useless. The SHA-256 authenticated token further ensures data integrity during transmission, preventing man-in-the-middle tampering. The dual-verification architecture, where both the client and server independently perform feature vector matching, provides an additional layer of defense: a compromised client cannot simply assert an identity without producing verifiable biometric evidence, and a compromised server cannot be tricked into accepting forged credentials without matching against the enrolled database. The any-match voting strategy across 30 independent feature extractors further raises the bar for attackers, as defeating a single extractor is insufficient to gain authentication.

Despite these protections, several attack vectors remain that warrant discussion. Physical spoofing attacks, where an adversary presents a photograph, video, or 3D mask to the camera, are not addressed by the disposable biometric framework alone. LBP-based facial recognition, like most 2D facial recognition systems, can be fooled by high-quality presentation attacks without additional liveness detection mechanisms. Incorporating liveness detection, such as requiring eye-blinking, head movement, or analyzing texture patterns indicative of living skin, would be necessary for deployment in high-security environments. Side-channel attacks, where an adversary monitors system behavior such as authentication timing or resource usage to infer information about enrolled users, present another concern. The current implementation does not include constant-time comparison or other timing attack mitigations. At the network level, an attacker who compromises a single WebID server could extract that user's FOAF relationships and limited profile attributes. While the hierarchical exposure model limits what can be learned, even partial relationship data could be used for targeted social engineering against connected members. Denial-of-service attacks against individual servers or the broader network could disrupt authentication and collaboration, as the decentralized system lacks the redundancy and load-balancing infrastructure typical of centralized cloud services. These considerations highlight that disposable biometrics, while a significant advancement, must be part of a broader defense-in-depth strategy.

## 7.5 Generalizability and Future Applicability

While the proposed system is demonstrated with facial recognition using LBP and GEFE, the architectural principles are modality-agnostic and could be extended to other biometric traits. Fingerprint recognition, voice authentication, iris scanning, or even behavioral biometrics such as keystroke dynamics could all be integrated using the same disposable feature extraction paradigm. The key requirement is that the biometric modality supports the generation of multiple, independent feature representations that can be discarded after each use. Voice, for example, could leverage Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs) with randomized filter bank configurations as disposable extractors. This flexibility makes the framework applicable to a wide range of deployment contexts, from mobile devices where camera quality may be limited to hands-free environments where voice authentication is preferable. The WebID infrastructure and RDF profile system remain unchanged regardless of the biometric modality, providing a consistent identity layer across diverse authentication methods.

Looking toward future applicability, the convergence of this work with blockchain and distributed ledger technologies presents a natural evolution. While the current system uses WebID-based decentralized identity without blockchain, the trust scores, authentication events, and task completion records could be immutably recorded on a ledger to provide even stronger auditability and non-repudiation. Smart contracts could automate task assignment, reward distribution, and access control decisions based on the AHP-weighted trust scores stored on-chain. Regulatory alignment is another critical consideration. The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in Europe, the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA), and similar frameworks worldwide emphasize data minimization, purpose limitation, and the right to erasure. The disposable biometric approach aligns naturally with these requirements, as biometric data is not persistently stored in a reconstructable form. However, reconciling the irreversibility of blockchain-based records with the right to erasure would require careful architectural choices, such as storing only hashed references on-chain while maintaining deletable data off-chain. As decentralized identity and self-sovereign identity standards mature, the principles demonstrated in this work could inform the design of next-generation collaborative platforms that prioritize user autonomy, trust-based cooperation, and privacy-preserving security.
